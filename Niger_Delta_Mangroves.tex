\abstract{
This paper addresses the issue of satellite image processing aimed at mapping the vegetation indices in mangrove forests of Niger Delta, southern Nigeria. In the last decade, the programming approaches were developed and integrated in geospatial studies. This paper proposes a GRASS GIS framework to calculate and visualise ten vegetation indices (VI) using scripting techniques of image processing. The methods include using the \emph{i.vi}, \emph{i.landsat.toar} and other modules of GRASS GIS. The estuary of the Niger Delta in the Gulf of Guinea is an essential hotspot of biodiversity in western coast of Africa. At the same time, recent issues of changing climate and the effects from the increasing population contribute to the vulnerability of coastal landscapes and present threats to ecosystem communities in southern Nigeria. In this paper, we prove that environmental changes in the coastal landscapes can be numerically modelled and evaluated using a time series analysis of the remote sensing data. We examined four Landsat 8-9 OLI/TIRS images covering the western segment of the Niger Delta in the Bight of Benin for years 2013, 2015, 2021 and 2022. The analysis of data showed variations in the vegetation health of the swamp forests of the Niger Delta over a decade. Variations in spectral reflectance also pointed at changes in urban spaces of the major cities (Onitsha, Sapele, Warri and Benin CIty) and smaller villages in the Delta State caused by recent urbanisation processes. Based on the GRASS GIS \emph{i.vi} module, ten VI are computed and mapped for the western segment of the Niger Delta estuary: Atmospherically Resistant Vegetation Index (ARVI), Green Atmospherically Resistant Vegetation Index (GARI), Green Vegetation Index (GVI), Difference Vegetation Index (DVI), Perpendicular Vegetation Index (PVI), Global Environmental Monitoring Index (GEMI), Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI), Second Modified Soil Adjusted Vegetation Index (MSAVI2), Infrared Percentage Vegetation Index (IPVI) and Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI). Experimental results show changes in the vegetation health, plant  greenness and coverage based on the image analysis performed using GRASS GIS. Finally, the technical functionality of the GRASS GIS demonstrated the best performance in terms of image processing and analysis.
}

\section{Introduction}

The coastal landscapes of southern Nigeria (Figure \ref{fig01}) are covered with various kinds of vegetation including mangroves in the Niger River Delta \cite{Ukpong1997}. Mangroves present a unique halophyte type of lowland forests  existing in saline waters of intertidal zone in tropics and subtropics. The distinct feature of mangroves that differs it from usual types of plants is that mangrove shrubs and trees have a complex root system with prop roots. Mangroves distributed in the Niger Delta are the most extensive of in West Africa \cite{KINAKO197735}. Salinity is the key factor playing major role in the dynamics of mangrove habitats \cite{Amadi,Okafor}. Other factors include the distance from the coast, tidal cycles and freshwater inflow, there are diverse types of mangrove swamp with species adapted to different salinity gradients \cite{Ukpong1991}. 

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
	\includegraphics[width=14.0 cm]{Fig_01.jpg}
	\caption{Topographic map of Nigeria. Software: Generic Mapping Tools (GMT) version 6.1.1, \cite{Wessel}. Data source: GEBCO/SRTM \cite{GEBCO,SRTM}. Cartography source: authors.
	\label{fig01}}
\end{figure}

Mangroves have a high environmental and socioeconomic value providing diverse important functions as habitat for species living in intertidal environment \cite{James}. At the same time, recently mangrove ecosystems became under the threat of extinction with the increasing rate of deforestation reported in many relevant papers \cite{Enaruvbe2016,Numbere,Osuji,Fasona}. The deforestation of mangrove forests has diverse reasons. These include the effects from climate change since the Niger Delta is the most climate-vulnerable region in Nigeria \cite{BALOGUN2022100304,hydrology7010019}. Other climate-related issues affecting mangrove distribution include droughts, flash floods, land subsidence, soil erosion, sea level rise and invasion of external species \cite{UKPONG199761}. 

Moreover, the anthropogenic activities also contribute to the extinction of mangroves. For instance, these include local road construction, housing and farming, or industrial works related to oil such as clearing waterfront vegetation \cite{Zabbey}. Further, \cite{Godstime} mentions the unsustainable economic development and the pollution of habitats with aquaculture reagents in the Niger Delta region which resulted in the decline of mangrove landscapes up to 35\% since 1960s due to the active development of aquaculture. For instance, at a global scale, \cite{Goldberg} reports 62\% of losses in mangroves between 2000 and 2016 due to the land-use change, mostly caused by land occupied by aquaculture and agriculture plantations. Finally, timber production, wood harvesting, conversion of mangroves for agriculture and bio-fuel plantations, and coastal development are other human-induced drivers responsible for the extinction of mangroves \cite{Feka}. All these factors critically threaten the mangroves at high rates thereby reducing the number of remaining spots of mangroves. The integrated effects from all these triggers including natural and human factors resulted in mangrove forests being among the most vulnerable marine ecosystems in West Africa \cite{ORIJEMIE2012360}. 

Remote sensing technologies and satellite imagery provide a valuable combination for evaluating area of mangrove distribution threatened to extinction, as well as monitoring the magnitudes of degrading \cite{Hu}. Using several satellite images as a time series helps to estimate the  extent to which coastal ecosystems and mangrove communities is declining through visualization and comparison of scenes taken for several consecutive years. Such time series of satellite images enable to perform change detection in land cover types through comparative analysis of vegetation indices, such as NDVI \cite{OCHEGE2017211,Lemenkova202229}. For instance, \cite{Enaruvbe2015} reports temporal variations in the intensity of change in land-use patterns in the Niger Delta based on the examined data for a roughly 3 decade period since 1980s to 2010s.

Previous studies  \cite{NUMBERE2022433,FASHAE2022e01286,Olorunfemi,Fagbeja} developed and applied methods based on using Geographic Information System (GIS) to quantitatively evaluate and map environmental degradation of land cover types and decline of vegetation. However, there is a certain deficiency in functionality of the traditional GIS such as slow-speed data processing, manual operational workflow and subjectivity in data analysis which may result in biased solutions to environmental mapping. In contrast, using scripts and programming techniques supports advanced approaches of mapping and accurate visualization of remote sensing data using machine based techniques \cite{Lemenkova202227,Lemenkova202230}. Such methods can be used for mapping changes in the mangrove areas and distribution of land cover types along the coasts of the Gulf of Guinea. 

In turn, detailed geospatial analysis of the the  environmental processes in coastal areas of West Africa supports to take measures of minimising the threats to mangroves and maintain balance in the human-nature existence for sustainable coastal development in Nigeria. Therefore, it becomes necessary to apply and practically evaluate the programming methods for remote sensing data processing and analysis to aid research on mangrove distribution through advanced cartographic tools. Hence, to achieve robustness in satellite image analysis, we further proposed the use of the GRASS GIS modules based on scripting approach for automation of spatial data processing.

This paper is a continuation of our previous work \cite{Lemenkova202301,Lemenkova202228} on the use of scripts for cartographic data processing, where a spatial data representation derived from the use of modules was proposed using console-based regime. Herein, we provide an extended application of scripts towards remote sensing data analysis, and propose the use of GRASS GIS for computing vegetation indices to achieve better performance compared to the traditional GIS in terms of both accuracy through automation of data processing, and computational costs due to open source availability of the GRASS GIS software.

This framework relies on the use of vegetation indices (VI) methods of assessment of vegetation health and greenness. These methods represent the data on plant canopy by means of computing the values of pixels in the target bands of the Landsat 8-9 OLI/TIRS images. Since spectral reflectance of land cover types vary significantly in diverse land cover types on various channels of Landsat, their combination enables to accurately calculate and visualise the distribution of the given landscapes over the area. The absorption and reflectance of solar radiation by various land cover types (water bodies, vegetation, urban spaces, deserts and sands, bare soil and rocks, etc) presents fundamental feature of remote sensing data analysis which enables to study characteristics of the Earth's landscapes using spaceborne data. The computation of the VI is most often based on the use of ratio and various combinations of Red and Near-Infrared (NIR) bands, though addition of other bands (such as Green and Blue, and sometimes SWIR) are also possible.

\subsection{Atmospherically Resistant Vegetation Index (ARVI)}

The computation of ARVI is based on the formula developed by \cite{134076,HUETE1997440}:

\begin{equation}
	ARVI = \frac{NIR - (2.0*Red - Blue)}{NIR + (2.0*Red - Blue)}
\end{equation}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the ARVI,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.3 -p
i.vi blue=lsat8_2013_toar.2 red=lsat8_2013_toar.4 nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 \
       viname=arvi output=lsat8_2013.arvi --overwrite
r.colors lsat8_2013.arvi color=bcyr -e
d.mon wx1
d.rast lsat8_2013.arvi
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.arvi range=-0.7,0.3 title="ARVI" title_fontsize=14 font=Helvetica fontsize=12 -t -s -b border_color=white thin=12 label_step=0.1 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_ARVI_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

%------------------------->
\subsection{Green Atmospherically Resistant Vegetation Index (GARI)}

The computation of GARI is based on the formula developed by \cite{GITELSON1996289}:

\begin{equation}
	GARI = \frac{NIR - (Green - (Blue - Red))}{NIR + (Green - (Blue - Red))}
\end{equation} 

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the GARI,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
# GARI = (nirchan - (2.0*redchan - bluechan)) / ( nirchan + (2.0*redchan - bluechan))
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.3 -p
i.vi blue=lsat8_2013_toar.2 green=lsat8_2013_toar.3 red=lsat8_2013_toar.4 \
     nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 viname=gari output=lsat8_2013.gari --overwrite
r.colors lsat8_2013.gari color=rainbow -e
d.mon wx2
d.rast lsat8_2013.gari
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.gari range=-0.5,0.7 title="GARI" title_fontsize=14 font="Helvetica" fontsize=12 -t -b bgcolor=white label_step=0.1 border_color=white thin=8 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_GARI_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Green Vegetation Index (GVI)}

The computation of Tasselled Cap Green Vegetation Index (GVI) is based on the formula developed by \cite{4157507}:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
	GVI = -0.2848 * Blue - 0.2435 * Green - 0.5436 * Red + \\
	0.7243 * NIR + 0.0840 * SWIR1- 0.1800 * SWIR2
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the GVI,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.3 -p
i.vi blue=lsat8_2013_toar.2 green=lsat8_2013_toar.3 red=lsat8_2013_toar.4 nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 band5=lsat8_2013_toar.6 band7=lsat8_2013_toar.7 viname=gvi output=lsat8_2013.gvi --overwrite
r.colors.stddev lsat8_2013.gvi
d.mon wx3
d.rast lsat8_2013.gvi
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.gvi range=-1,0.7 title="GVI" title_fontsize=14 font="Helvetica" fontsize=12 -t -b bgcolor=white label_step=0.1 border_color=white thin=8 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_GVI_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

%------------------------->
\subsection{Difference Vegetation Index (DVI)}

The use of DVI is based on the ratio between the NIR and Red channels \cite{Naji2018}:

\begin{equation}
	DVI = (NIR - Red)
\end{equation}

This is the original formula used later in a modified version for computing the NDVI which is based on the DVI in the adjusted way and applied in many works \cite{TUCKER1979237,RONDEAUX199695,DATT1998111}:
\begin{equation}
	NDVI = \frac{(NIR - Red)}{(NIR + Red)}
\end{equation}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the DVI,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.1 -p
i.vi blue=lsat8_2013_toar.2 red=lsat8_2013_toar.4 nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 viname=dvi output=lsat8_2013.dvi --overwrite
r.colors lsat8_2013.dvi color=bgyr -e
d.mon wx4
d.rast lsat8_2013.dvi
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.dvi range=-0.1,0.3 title="DVI" title_fontsize=14 font="Helvetica" fontsize=12 -t -b bgcolor=white label_step=0.02 border_color=white thin=8 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_DVI_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Perpendicular Vegetation Index (PVI)}

The use of PVI is based on the formula that includes the sine and cosine of the NIR and Red channels \cite{PERRY1984169}:

\begin{equation}
	PVI = sin(a)NIR-cos(a)Red
\end{equation} 

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the PVI,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.1 -p
i.vi red=lsat8_2013_toar.4 nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 viname=pvi output=lsat8_2013.pvi --overwrite
r.colors lsat8_2013.pvi color=bgyr -e
d.mon wx5
d.rast lsat8_2013.pvi
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.pvi range=-0.1,0.3 title="PVI" title_fontsize=14 font="Helvetica" fontsize=12 -t -b bgcolor=white label_step=0.02 border_color=white thin=8 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_PVI_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Global Environmental Monitoring Index (GEMI)}

\begin{align*}
GEMI = \Bigl( \frac{2*((NIR * NIR)-(Red * Red)) +
       1.5*NIR+0.5*Red}{NIR + Red + 0.5} * \\
        (1 - 0.25 * 
       \frac{2*[(NIR * NIR)-(Red * Red) +
       1.5*NIR+0.5*Red]}{NIR + Red + 0.5} \Bigr ) -
       \frac{Red - 0.125}{1 - Red}
\end{align*}

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the GEMI,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
# Calculation of GEMI: Global Environmental Monitoring Index
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.1 -p
i.vi red=lsat8_2013_toar.4 nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 viname=gemi output=lsat8_2013.gemi --overwrite
r.colors lsat8_2013.gemi color=roygbiv -e
d.erase
d.rast lsat8_2013.gemi
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.gemi range=-0.5,1.0 title="GEMI" title_fontsize=14 font="Helvetica" fontsize=12 -t -b bgcolor=white label_step=0.1 border_color=white thin=8 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_GEMI_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI)}

The original formula of the NDWI has been developed by \cite{McFeeters} as a ratio of Green and NIR channels: 
\begin{equation}
NDWI = \frac{green - NIR}{green + NIR}
\end{equation} 

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the NDWI,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.1 -p
i.vi green=lsat8_2013_toar.3 nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 viname=ndwi output=lsat8_2013.ndwi --overwrite
r.colors lsat8_2013.ndwi color=viridis -e
d.mon wx0
d.rast lsat8_2013.ndwi
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.ndwi range=-1.0,0.5 title="NDWI" title_fontsize=14 font="Helvetica" fontsize=12 -t -b bgcolor=white label_step=0.1 border_color=white thin=8 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_NDWI_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Second Modified Soil Adjusted Vegetation Index (MSAVI2)}

\begin{equation}
	MSAVI2 = \frac{1}{2}*\Bigl(2*NIR+1-\sqrt{(2*NIR+1)^2-8*(NIR-red)}\Bigr)
\end{equation} 

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the MSAVI2,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.1 -p
i.vi red=lsat8_2013_toar.4 nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 viname=msavi2 output=lsat8_2013.msavi2 --overwrite
r.colors lsat8_2013.msavi2 color=soilmoisture -e
d.mon wx0
d.rast lsat8_2013.msavi2
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.msavi2 range=-1.0,0.5 title="MSAVI2" title_fontsize=14 font="Helvetica" fontsize=12 -t -b bgcolor=white label_step=0.1 border_color=white thin=8 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_MSAVI2_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Infrared Percentage Vegetation Index (IPVI)}

\begin{equation}
	IPVI = \frac{NIR}{(NIR+Red)}
\end{equation} 

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the IPVI,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.1 -p
i.vi red=lsat8_2013_toar.4 nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 viname=ipvi output=lsat8_2013.ipvi --overwrite
r.colors lsat8_2013.ipvi color=haxby -e
d.mon wx0
d.rast lsat8_2013.ipvi
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.ipvi range=-1.0,1.0 title="IPVI" title_fontsize=14 font="Helvetica" fontsize=12 -t -b bgcolor=white label_step=0.1 border_color=white thin=8 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_IPVI_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI)}

\begin{equation}
EVI = 2.5 * \frac{NIR - Red}{NIR + 6.0 * Red - 7.5 * Blue + 1.0}
\end{equation} 

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash,caption=GRASS GIS script for computing the EVI,style=mystyle,label={lst01}]
g.region raster=lsat8_2013_toar.1 -p
i.vi blue=lsat8_2013_toar.2 red=lsat8_2013_toar.4 nir=lsat8_2013_toar.5 viname=evi output=lsat8_2013.evi --overwrite
r.colors lsat8_2013.evi color=elevation -e
d.mon wx0
d.rast lsat8_2013.evi
d.legend raster=lsat8_2013.evi range=-1.0,1.0 title="EVI" title_fontsize=14 font="Helvetica" fontsize=12 -t -b bgcolor=white label_step=0.1 border_color=white thin=8 -d
d.out.file output=Nigeria_EVI_2013 format=jpg --overwrite
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Perpendicular Vegetation Index (PVI)}

The PVI index (Figure \ref{fig07}) computes the perpendicular distance of the pixel from the soil line which linearly correlates with the area covered by vegetation canopy. It corrects for the difference between soil background which works best for arid and semiarid regions. However, for Nigeria with its tropical climate and dense swamp forests, it is less effective and suitable compared to IPVI and MSAVI2 that better show the difference in state of the mangrove forests. The higher PVI values (shown as red colors) point at the effects from brighter soil background in the agricultural areas in the Edo State. The disadvantages of the PVI consists in the atmospheric effects where it performs less accurate compared to ARVI and GARI.

\section{Discussion}
In this paper, we have applied and proposed a GRASS GIS framework for satellite image processing and analysis using Landsat 8-9 OLI/TIRS scenes for the tasks of computing VI on the western segment of Niger River Delta, southern Nigeria, the Gulf of Guinea.  Ten VI computed and visualised using GRASS GIS scripts are demonstrated as a comparative analysis of the environmental changes in the vegetation state: ARVI, GARI, GVI, DVI, PVI, GEMI, NDWI, MSAVI2, IPVI and EVI. 

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusions}
In spite of their environmental value, mangrove forests located along the African coasts of the Atlantic Sea are currently subject to a range of natural threats. To evaluate changes in land cover types covered by mangrove swamps in southern Nigeria, this paper visualised ten vegetation indices using remote sensing data by scripting techniques of GRASS GIS. 

In this paper, we have proposed a GRASS GIS framework for image processing and analysis in which computing vegetation images using algorithms embedded in 'i.ivi' and scripting approach run from the console are integrated consistently for comparative analysis of time series of the Landsat 8-9 OLI/TIRS images covering Niger Delta, Nigeria. To find variations in the distribution of mangroves landscapes and to separate them against other land cover types, we applied ten diverse vegetation indices and compared the performance results. To produce colour-consistent images, the maps derived from the computed VI were plotted in the identical colour ranges in each set of the four images, for comparison of changes. The algorithms of the GRASS GIS demonstrated powerful functionality in satellite image processing and demonstrated accurately produced maps resulted from the calculated VI in western segment of the Niger Delta for years 2013, 2015, 2021 and 2022.
